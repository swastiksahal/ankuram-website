What is a Vector?

Let's start with a simple situation.

Your friend calls you and asks, "When will you reach my house?"

What do you think about before answering?

Two things:

  1. How fast am I going? (Are you on a bike doing 40 km/h, or stuck in traffic barely moving?)
  2. Which direction am I heading? (Are you going towards his house, or are you on the wrong road going the opposite way?)

If you're going 60 km/h but in the wrong direction, you're not getting any closer. In fact, you're getting farther. Your speed doesn't matter if your direction is wrong.

So when you answer your friend, you're actually calculating two things in your head — your speed AND your direction relative to his house.

This is the idea behind a vector.

The Definition

A vector is any quantity that has both magnitude (how much) and direction (which way).

A scalar is a quantity that has only magnitude. Direction doesn't apply to it.

How to Identify Vectors vs Scalars

Here's a simple test. Ask yourself: "Does it make sense to ask 'in which direction' for this quantity?"

Temperature is 30°C. In which direction? The question doesn't make sense. Temperature is just a number. → Scalar

Force is 50 N. In which direction? Yes, this matters! A push to the right is different from a push to the left. → Vector

Mass is 10 kg. In which direction? Meaningless. Mass is just a number. → Scalar

Velocity is 20 m/s. In which direction? Absolutely matters! 20 m/s north vs 20 m/s south are completely different. → Vector

Examples from Daily Life

Velocity vs Speed

You're driving at 50 km/h. That's your speed — just a number, a scalar.

But if I say you're driving at 50 km/h towards Hyderabad, now I've given direction too. This is velocity — a vector.

Force

You push a heavy table with 100 N of force. But push it in which direction? Towards the wall? Away from the wall?

If two people push the same table with equal force but in opposite directions, the table doesn't move. The forces cancel out. Direction matters.

Displacement vs Distance

You walked 2 km. But 2 km in which direction?

If you walked 1 km north and then 1 km south, you're back where you started. Your total distance is 2 km, but your displacement is zero.

Distance is scalar (total ground covered). Displacement is vector (where you ended up relative to where you started).

How Do We Represent Vectors?

A vector has magnitude and direction. We need a way to show both.

The simplest way: an arrow.

  • Length of the arrow → magnitude (how much)
  • Where the arrow points → direction (which way)

When you draw an arrow on paper:

  • The starting point is called the tail
  • The ending point (the tip) is called the head
  • The arrow points from tail to head

A Simple Example

Suppose you walk 4 km towards the east.

You can represent this on paper:

  • Pick a scale: let's say 1 cm = 1 km
  • Draw an arrow 4 cm long, pointing to the right (east)

That arrow IS your displacement vector. It captures both the 4 km (length) and the east direction (where it points).

Now suppose your friend walks 4 km towards the north.

His arrow is also 4 cm long, but it points upward (north).

Same magnitude, different direction. Two different vectors.

Notation — How Do We Write Vectors?

When we write vectors, we need to distinguish them from regular numbers.

Arrow notation:

$\vec{a}$ or $\vec{AB}$

The small arrow on top tells you "this is a vector, not just a number."

$\vec{AB}$ means "the vector going from point A to point B."

Bold notation:

a or F

In printed books, vectors are often written in bold.

Magnitude notation:

$|\vec{a}|$ or just $a$

When you want only the magnitude (without direction), you write $|\vec{a}|$ or simply $a$.

For example:

  • $\vec{F}$ = force vector (has magnitude and direction)
  • $|\vec{F}|$ or $F$ = magnitude of force (just a number, like 50 N)

Position Vectors

Suppose you have a point P somewhere in space. How do you describe where P is?

You pick a reference point called the origin (O). Then you draw an arrow from O to P.

This arrow is called the position vector of P.

It tells you: starting from the origin, how far and in which direction is point P?

We write it as $\vec{OP}$ or $\vec{r}$.

Equal Vectors

Two vectors are equal if they have:

  1. Same magnitude
  2. Same direction

They don't have to start from the same point.

If you draw two arrows of the same length pointing the same way, but in different parts of the page, they represent the same vector.

Negative of a Vector

The negative of a vector $\vec{a}$ is written as $-\vec{a}$.

It has:

  • Same magnitude as $\vec{a}$
  • Opposite direction

If $\vec{a}$ points east, then $-\vec{a}$ points west with the same length.

Zero Vector

The zero vector, written as $\vec{0}$, has:

  • Magnitude = 0
  • Direction = undefined (or any direction, doesn't matter)

It's like standing still — no displacement in any direction.

Solved Illustrations

Illustration 1: Identifying Vectors and Scalars

Question: Classify the following as vectors or scalars:
(a) 5 m/s²
(b) 10 N towards north
(c) 273 K
(d) 50 kg
(e) 30 m/s eastward

Solution:

(a) 5 m/s² — This is just a magnitude. No direction specified. But acceleration is inherently a vector quantity. As written here, only magnitude is given, so this represents the magnitude of acceleration (scalar representation). If we say "5 m/s² downward," it becomes a vector.

(b) 10 N towards north — Force with direction specified. Vector.

(c) 273 K — Temperature. Direction doesn't apply. Scalar.

(d) 50 kg — Mass. Direction doesn't apply. Scalar.

(e) 30 m/s eastward — Speed with direction = velocity. Vector.

Illustration 2: Understanding Displacement vs Distance

Question: A person walks 3 km north, then 4 km east. Find:
(a) Total distance covered
(b) Magnitude of displacement
(c) Direction of displacement

Solution:

(a) Distance = total path length = 3 + 4 = 7 km

(b) Displacement is the straight line from start to finish.

Draw it: 3 km north, then 4 km east forms a right angle.

The displacement is the hypotenuse:

\[|\vec{d}| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = \textbf{5 km}\]

(c) Direction:

\[\tan\theta = \frac{\text{east component}}{\text{north component}} = \frac{4}{3}\]

\[\theta = \tan^{-1}(1.33) \approx 53.1°\]

Direction: 53.1° east of north (or 36.9° north of east)

Illustration 3: Position Vector

Question: A point P has coordinates (3, 4) in a 2D plane. Write the position vector of P with respect to origin. Also find its magnitude.

Solution:

Position vector of P:

\[\vec{OP} = 3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j}\]

(We'll learn about $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$ in detail in Lecture 2, but for now, $\hat{i}$ means "along x-axis" and $\hat{j}$ means "along y-axis")

Magnitude:

\[|\vec{OP}| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = \textbf{5 units}\]

Illustration 4: Equal Vectors

Question: Vector $\vec{a}$ goes from point (1, 2) to point (4, 6). Vector $\vec{b}$ goes from point (0, 0) to point (3, 4). Are $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ equal?

Solution:

For vectors to be equal, they must have same magnitude AND same direction.

Vector $\vec{a}$:

  • Components: $(4-1, 6-2) = (3, 4)$
  • Magnitude: $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$
  • Direction: $\tan^{-1}(4/3) \approx 53.1°$ with x-axis

Vector $\vec{b}$:

  • Components: $(3-0, 4-0) = (3, 4)$
  • Magnitude: $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$
  • Direction: $\tan^{-1}(4/3) \approx 53.1°$ with x-axis

Same magnitude ✓
Same direction ✓

Yes, $\vec{a} = \vec{b}$

Note: They start from different points, but that doesn't matter for equality. Only magnitude and direction matter.

Illustration 5: Negative Vector

Question: If $\vec{a} = 5$ units pointing 30° above the positive x-axis, describe $-\vec{a}$.

Solution:

$-\vec{a}$ has:

  • Same magnitude: 5 units
  • Opposite direction: 30° below the negative x-axis (or equivalently, 210° from positive x-axis)

In other words, if $\vec{a}$ points to the upper right, $-\vec{a}$ points to the lower left.

Illustration 6: Resultant Displacement

Question: A bird flies 100 m east, then 100 m north, then 100 m west. What is the bird's displacement from its starting point?

Solution:

Let's track the position:

  • Start: Origin (0, 0)
  • After 100 m east: (100, 0)
  • After 100 m north: (100, 100)
  • After 100 m west: (0, 100)

Final position: (0, 100)
Starting position: (0, 0)

Displacement vector: $(0-0, 100-0) = (0, 100)$

Magnitude: $\sqrt{0^2 + 100^2} = \textbf{100 m}$

Direction: Due north (along positive y-axis)

Interesting: The bird flew 300 m total (distance), but ended up only 100 m from the start (displacement).

Key Takeaways

  • A vector has both magnitude and direction. A scalar has only magnitude.
  • Test: "Does 'in which direction' make sense?" If yes → vector. If no → scalar.
  • Vectors are represented as arrows. Length = magnitude, arrowhead = direction.
  • Position vector of a point P is the arrow from origin O to P.
  • Two vectors are equal if they have same magnitude AND same direction (starting point doesn't matter).
  • Negative of a vector has same magnitude but opposite direction.
  • Zero vector has zero magnitude and undefined direction.
  • Distance is scalar (total path). Displacement is vector (start to end).
